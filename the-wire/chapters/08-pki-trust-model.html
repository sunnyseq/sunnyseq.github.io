The PKI trust model

Part II ended on an unresolved problem: a public key is a number, and a number has no name. This chapter is about the machinery the internet built to attach names to numbers — and about how much of that machinery is institutional rather than mathematical.

8.1 The problem, stated exactly

Chapter 6 gave two strangers a shared secret over a hostile network. Chapter 7 let one party prove authorship to anyone. Neither answers the question a browser actually needs answered:

This connection is cryptographically sound. Is it sound with my bank, or with someone standing between me and my bank?

An attacker in the middle can run a flawless key exchange, sign the transcript flawlessly, and present a perfectly valid certificate — their own. Every cryptographic check passes. The mathematics is working correctly and the answer is still wrong, because mathematics does not know who anyone is.

Binding a key to a name requires someone to assert the binding, and requires you to have a reason to believe that someone. There are only three structural ways to do this, and the internet chose the third.

Why the web of trust lost

PGP's model is more decentralised and philosophically appealing, and it does not work for the web. To verify a stranger's key you need a path of signatures from someone you already trust, and for a first visit to an arbitrary site that path almost never exists. It also pushes a judgement onto every user — how much do I trust this signer? — which is not a decision people can make billions of times a day. PKI's answer is blunt: a few hundred organisations vouch for everyone, and your operating system vendor decides which ones. Less elegant, and it actually functions.

8.2 The chain

A certificate is a signed statement: this public key belongs to this name, and I, the issuer, attest to it. Since verifying that signature needs the issuer's public key, you need a certificate for the issuer too — and so on, until you reach a key you already trust for reasons outside the chain.

That terminal key is a root, and it is self-signed. A self-signed certificate proves nothing cryptographically; its signature is a statement by the root about itself, which anyone can produce. Roots are trusted because they are already in your trust store, put there by Apple, Microsoft, Google, Mozilla or your distribution, after an audit process. That is the bottom of the stack, and it is a human institution, not a theorem.

Validation runs from the leaf upwards, but trust flows from the root downwards. Every step must succeed; there is no partial credit and no "mostly valid" chain.

8.3 What a client actually checks

"Validate the chain" is shorthand for a specific sequence, standardised in RFC 5280 §6. A client performs all of it, in order, and any failure is fatal.

  1. Build a path. Starting from the leaf, find a certificate whose Subject matches this one's Issuer, and repeat until reaching something in the trust store. Note the servers do not always send a usable path — see below.
  2. Verify each signature. Each certificate's signature must verify under the next certificate's public key.
  3. Check validity periods. Every certificate in the path must be currently within notBefore…notAfter. The client's clock is the authority, which is why a wrong system date breaks the whole web.
  4. Check basic constraints. Every non-leaf must have CA:TRUE, and must satisfy any pathLenConstraint.
  5. Check key usage. CAs need keyCertSign; leaves need digitalSignature or keyEncipherment, and typically serverAuth in extended key usage.
  6. Check name constraints. If a CA is constrained to .example.com, a certificate it issues for anything else is invalid.
  7. Check revocation status. CRL or OCSP — Chapter 11, and the weakest link in practice.
  8. Check the name matches. The hostname you asked for must appear in the leaf's Subject Alternative Name. This is a separate step from chain validation and is the one most often botched in custom clients.
Step 8 is where homegrown clients fail

A great many libraries have shipped code that verified the chain perfectly and never checked whether the certificate was actually for the host being contacted. An attacker then presents a valid, properly-issued certificate for a domain they legitimately own, and the client accepts it for your bank. Chain validity and name matching are independent checks, and skipping the second silently defeats the entire system. If you ever find yourself passing InsecureSkipVerify, verify=False or -k, this is the check you are switching off.

8.4 Why intermediates exist

Root private keys are extraordinarily valuable and correspondingly awkward. They live in an HSM, in a safe, in a room with cameras, and are brought out for ceremonies witnessed by auditors. You cannot use a key like that to sign millions of certificates a day.

So roots sign a small number of intermediates, and intermediates do the day-to-day work. The benefits are structural:

  • The root stays offline. An online key is an exposed key.
  • Compromise is survivable. A compromised intermediate can be revoked. A compromised root cannot, in any practical sense — removing it from trust stores takes years to propagate, and DigiNotar in 2011 showed what that looks like: a CA breached, fraudulent Google certificates issued and used against Iranian users, and the company bankrupt within a month.
  • Roots outlive their algorithms. Roots have 20–25 year lifetimes. Intermediates rotate every few years, letting the CA move to new key sizes and signature algorithms without touching the trust store.
  • Constraint. Intermediates can be name-constrained or purpose-constrained in ways the root is not.

The chain the server must send

A server sends its leaf plus every intermediate needed to reach a root — but not the root itself, since the client already has it and sending it wastes bytes on every handshake. Omitting an intermediate is one of the most common TLS misconfigurations, and it is insidious because it often works on your machine: browsers cache intermediates they have seen before, and some clients will fetch a missing one via the Authority Information Access extension. So the site works for you and fails for a fresh client, a mobile app, or a `curl` in CI.

Cross-signing, and why chains sometimes look odd

A new CA's root is not in any trust store yet, and waiting for it to propagate takes years. The workaround is cross-signing: an established root also signs the new root, giving it two parents. Clients with the new root in their store use the short path; older clients follow the cross-signed path to a root they already have. Let's Encrypt did this via IdenTrust's DST Root CA X3, and when that cross-signature expired in September 2021 a wave of older Android devices and OpenSSL 1.0.2 clients broke — the certificates were fine, but those clients could not build a path to a root they trusted. It is worth remembering that a chain is not a list; it is a graph, and different clients may walk it differently.

8.5 The uncomfortable part

Any CA in your trust store can issue a certificate for any domain. There is no partitioning by default — the CA that issues certificates for a regional government can issue one for your bank, and your browser will accept it. That is roughly 130–150 organisations across dozens of jurisdictions, each with intermediates, each with employees.

Consequences that have actually happened:

  • DigiNotar (2011) — compromised; over 500 fraudulent certificates issued, including for *.google.com, used in bulk interception of Iranian users. Removed from all trust stores; company dissolved.
  • TÜRKTRUST (2013) — mistakenly issued intermediate CA certificates instead of leaf certificates. One was used to MITM Google traffic.
  • Symantec (2015–2018) — a sustained pattern of misissuance, including test certificates for domains they did not control. Google progressively distrusted the entire root hierarchy, forcing the reissuance of a substantial share of the web's certificates.
  • Enterprise interception — an employer installs its own root on managed devices, then a proxy transparently decrypts all TLS. Not an attack; a product. But it is the same mechanism, and it is why "there's a padlock" is a weaker statement than it appears.

Three mitigations exist, and none removes the underlying trust:

  • CAA records (Ch. 10) — a DNS record naming which CAs may issue for your domain. Binding on CAs, checked at issuance time.
  • Certificate Transparency (Ch. 11) — every issued certificate must appear in public append-only logs, so misissuance is detectable after the fact. This is what caught Symantec.
  • Pinning — an application hard-codes the acceptable key or CA. Powerful and dangerous: HTTP Public Key Pinning was deprecated because sites bricked themselves. It survives inside mobile apps, where the pin ships with the code and can be updated with it.

8.6 Private PKI

Internal systems frequently run their own CA, and this is a good idea rather than a compromise. A private root is distributed to your own machines, issues certificates only for your own services, and nobody else's trust store is involved — so the "any CA can issue for any domain" problem disappears entirely within that scope.

This is precisely how service mesh mTLS works (Ch. 16, 22): a cluster-internal CA issues short-lived certificates identifying workloads rather than hostnames, rotated every few hours, with no public CA anywhere in the picture. The trade is that you now operate a CA — root key custody, rotation, and revocation become your problem.

8.7 Lab

Shell · inspect and break real chains
# See the full chain a server sends, and how the client resolves it
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com -showcerts </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | grep -E '^ *[0-9]+ s:|^ *i:'
#   s: = subject, i: = issuer. Read it as a chain: each s: should match the next i:.

# Verification result, in one line
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 </dev/null 2>/dev/null | grep -E 'Verify return|Verification'

# Does the server send a COMPLETE chain? Verify with no local intermediate cache.
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -verify_return_error \
  -CAfile /etc/ssl/certs/ca-certificates.crt </dev/null 2>&1 | tail -5
#   "unable to get local issuer certificate" = a missing intermediate,
#   even though your browser probably renders the site fine.

# Build a two-level private PKI in one go
openssl req -x509 -newkey rsa:4096 -sha256 -days 3650 -nodes \
  -keyout ca.key -out ca.crt -subj "/CN=Field Notes Root CA" \
  -addext "basicConstraints=critical,CA:TRUE" -addext "keyUsage=critical,keyCertSign,cRLSign"

openssl req -newkey rsa:2048 -nodes -keyout srv.key -out srv.csr -subj "/CN=svc.internal"
openssl x509 -req -in srv.csr -CA ca.crt -CAkey ca.key -CAcreateserial -days 90 -sha256 \
  -extfile <(printf "basicConstraints=critical,CA:FALSE\nkeyUsage=critical,digitalSignature,keyEncipherment\nextendedKeyUsage=serverAuth\nsubjectAltName=DNS:svc.internal") \
  -out srv.crt

# Verify against your own root — then watch it fail against the public store
openssl verify -CAfile ca.crt srv.crt          # OK
openssl verify srv.crt                          # unable to get local issuer certificate

# Prove step 8 is independent: valid chain, wrong name
openssl verify -CAfile ca.crt -verify_hostname wrong.internal srv.crt
openssl verify -CAfile ca.crt -verify_hostname svc.internal   srv.crt

Knowledge check

Why is a root certificate self-signed, and what does that signature prove?

A self-signature is a statement by a key about itself, and anyone can make one for any name. The chain has to terminate somewhere, and it terminates in a decision made outside cryptography: your OS or browser vendor put that certificate in your store after an audit. That is the bottom of the trust stack.

A site works in your browser but fails with curl and in a mobile app. The certificate is not expired. What is the most likely cause?

Browsers cache intermediates they have seen and some will fetch missing ones via Authority Information Access. Fresh clients do neither, so an incomplete chain works for you and fails for everyone arriving cold. Test with -verify_return_error against a clean store rather than trusting your own browser.

What does the basicConstraints: CA:TRUE extension prevent?

Without it, anyone with a legitimate certificate for their own site could sign a certificate for your bank, and clients would build a valid path. Restricting issuance to a CA's own domain is nameConstraints, a different extension — and a rarely-used one.

Your organisation runs a private CA for internal service mTLS. Which problem does this genuinely solve?

Internal clients trust only your root, so the public CA population is irrelevant to them — the blast radius shrinks to one CA you control. Everything else still applies, and you have taken on root key custody and revocation as new responsibilities.

8.8 Carry forward

  • Cryptography binds a message to a key. Only an assertion binds a key to a name.
  • Validation runs leaf to root; trust flows root to leaf. Every step is mandatory.
  • Hostname verification is a separate check from chain validation, and skipping it is the classic silent failure.
  • Intermediates exist so the root can stay offline and a compromise can be survived.
  • Any public CA can issue for any domain. CAA, Certificate Transparency and pinning reduce that risk; none removes it.
