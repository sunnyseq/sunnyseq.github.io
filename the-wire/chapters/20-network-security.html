Network security

Chapter 2 put an attacker at points on a path. This chapter is about the controls that go at those points — which layer each one attaches to, what it can and cannot see there, and why the industry spent thirty years defending a perimeter before concluding there wasn't one.

20.1 Where security attaches

The layer a control lives at determines what it can see and what it can protect. Get this wrong and you deploy something that cannot possibly do what you wanted.

The recurring mistake is placing a control at a layer that cannot see what you asked it to inspect — a network firewall cannot read an encrypted payload, and an application check cannot stop a packet that never reaches it.

20.2 Firewalls, and what they actually decide

TypeDecides onBlind to

StatefulThe above plus connection state, so replies to outbound flows are allowedPayload contents
Application / L7HTTP methods, paths, headers, bodiesAnything encrypted, unless it terminates TLS
Identity-awareThe authenticated workload or userNothing relevant — but requires an identity system (Ch. 16)

Two things worth stating plainly. Egress filtering is usually more valuable than ingress filtering, and almost nobody does it. Ingress rules stop the initial intrusion, which is the part you were already thinking about. Egress rules stop the parts you were not: command-and-control callbacks, data exfiltration, and SSRF reaching your metadata endpoint (Ch. 18). Every serious breach involves data leaving.

And a firewall rule is an assertion about topology, not identity. "10.0.4.0/24 may reach the database" is true only while that range contains what you think it contains — which in a cluster with recycled pod IPs may be true for minutes.

20.3 Where the rules actually run

On Linux, packet filtering happens in netfilter hooks inside the kernel. Understanding the hook order explains a great deal of otherwise baffling behaviour:

                                  ┌─────────────┐
   PREROUTING ──► routing ──────► │   FORWARD   │ ──► POSTROUTING ──► out
   (DNAT)         decision   │    └─────────────┘         (SNAT)
                             │
                             ▼
                          INPUT ──► local process ──► OUTPUT
                                                        │
                                                        ▼
                                                  routing again

Destination NAT happens in PREROUTING, before the routing decision — which is why a rule matching the original destination address in INPUT will never fire once DNAT has rewritten it. This is exactly the machinery a Kubernetes Service uses: kube-proxy writes DNAT rules translating a virtual ClusterIP into a real pod IP, and conntrack keeps the reverse mapping so replies find their way back.

Conntrack is a table with a size

Stateful filtering means the kernel keeps an entry per flow. That table is finite, and when it fills, new connections are dropped — with a log line most people have never read. Under a SYN flood, or simply at high connection churn, `nf_conntrack: table full, dropping packet` is a self-inflicted outage that looks exactly like a network fault. Size it deliberately on anything handling significant connection rates.

WireGuard is the interesting case for this course, because its design applies every lesson from Part IV. No negotiation at all — one key exchange, one cipher, one hash. Removing choice removes downgrade attacks, exactly as TLS 1.3 concluded (Ch. 13). It is silent to unauthenticated packets, so it does not respond to scans. And it is small enough to audit, which after Heartbleed (Ch. 17) is a security property in itself.

The honest caveat about VPNs generally: a VPN moves the trust boundary, it does not remove it. Your traffic is protected from the local network and fully visible to the VPN operator. For a corporate tunnel that is the intent. For a consumer product advertising privacy, you have swapped an ISP you have a contract with for a company you know nothing about.

20.5 DNS

DNS is unauthenticated and unencrypted by default, and those are two separate problems with two separate solutions that people constantly confuse.

  • DNSSEC signs records, providing authenticity. It proves an answer came from the zone owner and was not forged. It does not encrypt anything — every query and answer remains fully visible.
  • DoH / DoT encrypt the query between you and your resolver, providing confidentiality on that hop. They say nothing about whether the resolver's answer is genuine, and the resolver still sees every name you look up.

They compose: DNSSEC for authenticity end to end, DoH or DoT for confidentiality on the first hop. Neither hides your queries from the resolver operator, which is the trade DoH makes — moving visibility from your ISP to whoever runs the resolver your browser chose.

Two DNS records this course has already leaned on: CAA restricting which CAs may issue for your domain (Ch. 10), and the HTTPS resource record carrying ECH keys (Ch. 14). Both make DNS part of your certificate security posture, which is worth remembering when deciding who can edit your zone.

20.6 Zero Trust

The perimeter model assumed a trusted inside and a hostile outside. Three things dissolved it: cloud services that live outside your network, remote work that puts users outside it, and lateral movement that makes "inside" meaningless once one host falls.

Zero Trust replaces location-based trust with identity-based trust. The principles, stripped of vendor language:

  • No implicit trust from network position. Being on the corporate LAN or inside the VPC grants nothing.
  • Authenticate and authorise every request, not every session, and not at the perimeter (Ch. 19).
  • Identity is the workload or the user, cryptographically proven — which in practice means mTLS with workload certificates (Ch. 16).
  • Encrypt everything in transit, including internal traffic. Chapter 1's TB-2.
  • Least privilege, continuously evaluated, with device posture and context feeding the decision.
  • Assume breach. Design so that one compromised workload yields as little as possible.
What Zero Trust is not

It is not a product, and buying a proxy does not deliver it. It is also not an argument for abandoning network controls — defence in depth still applies, and network policy remains one of the cheapest, most reliable layers you have precisely because it fails independently of your application code (Ch. 1, 18). The change is that the network is no longer permitted to be the only control, or the basis of an identity claim.

20.7 Lab

Shell · inspect the stack
# What is listening, and on which interface? 0.0.0.0 means the world.
ss -tlnp 2>/dev/null | awk 'NR==1 || /LISTEN/'

# Netfilter rules as the kernel sees them, by hook
sudo nft list ruleset 2>/dev/null | head -40 \
  || sudo iptables -L -n -v --line-numbers

# NAT rules \u2014 in a Kubernetes node this is mostly kube-proxy's work (Ch. 22)
sudo iptables -t nat -L PREROUTING -n | head -20

# Conntrack: current usage against the limit. A full table drops new connections.
cat /proc/sys/net/netfilter/nf_conntrack_count /proc/sys/net/netfilter/nf_conntrack_max 2>/dev/null
sudo conntrack -L 2>/dev/null | head -5

# Trace the path and see where control changes hands
mtr -rwc 10 example.com 2>/dev/null || traceroute example.com

# DNS: is the answer authenticated? Look for the 'ad' flag.
dig +dnssec +multi example.com A | grep -E 'flags:|RRSIG'
dig +dnssec cloudflare.com A | grep -c RRSIG
#   'ad' in the flags = your resolver validated DNSSEC. No RRSIG = zone unsigned.

# Is your query encrypted on the first hop? Compare plain DNS with DoH.
sudo tcpdump -i any -n 'port 53' -c 5 & sleep 1; dig +short example.com; sleep 1; kill %1
curl -s -H 'accept: application/dns-json' \
  'https://cloudflare-dns.com/dns-query?name=example.com&type=A' | python3 -m json.tool
#   The first is readable on the wire. The second is inside TLS.

# What does your resolver actually do?
resolvectl status 2>/dev/null | grep -E 'DNS Servers|DNSSEC|DNSOverTLS' || cat /etc/resolv.conf

# WireGuard: the entire configuration surface, which is the point
wg genkey | tee priv.key | wg pubkey > pub.key
cat <<'CONF'
[Interface]
PrivateKey = 
Address    = 10.10.0.2/32
[Peer]
PublicKey  =           # pinned. no negotiation, no CA, no cipher list.
AllowedIPs = 10.10.0.0/24
Endpoint   = vpn.example:51820
PersistentKeepalive = 25
CONF

# Egress: can this host reach things it should not?
for t in 169.254.169.254 10.0.0.1 8.8.8.8; do
  printf '%-18s ' "$t"
  timeout 2 bash -c "echo > /dev/tcp/$t/80" 2>/dev/null && echo REACHABLE || echo blocked
done
#   169.254.169.254 reachable from an app that fetches user URLs is the
#   Capital One breach waiting to happen (Ch. 18).

Knowledge check

What does DNSSEC provide that DoH does not?

Two different properties. DNSSEC signs records so a forged answer is detectable, and encrypts nothing. DoH encrypts the query to your resolver and says nothing about whether the answer is genuine. They compose, and neither hides your queries from the resolver itself.

Why is egress filtering often more valuable than ingress filtering?

Ingress rules address the intrusion you were already thinking about. Egress rules address everything after it, and every serious breach involves data leaving. Blocking an application from reaching the metadata endpoint turns an SSRF bug into a non-event without requiring the code to be correct.

Why does WireGuard deliberately offer no cryptographic negotiation?

Every legacy option retained for compatibility is something an attacker can steer you toward (Ch. 2, 13). WireGuard fixes one key exchange, one cipher and one hash, and versions the whole protocol if they need to change. The small codebase is a second security property — after Heartbleed, auditability counts.

A firewall rule allows 10.0.4.0/24 to reach the database. What is the fundamental weakness?

The rule is only as meaningful as the stability of what lives in that range, and in a cluster with recycled pod IPs that may be minutes. It also grants access to anything that lands there, compromised or not. Zero Trust's answer is to authenticate the workload cryptographically (Ch. 16) and keep the network rule as an independent second layer.

20.8 Carry forward

  • A control can only act on what its layer can see. Match the control to the layer.
  • Egress filtering constrains the attacker after a compromise, and is widely neglected.
  • DNAT happens before routing; conntrack tables are finite and fill.
  • A VPN relocates trust rather than removing it.
  • DNSSEC is authenticity, DoH is confidentiality. Different problems.
  • Zero Trust replaces location-based trust with proven identity — and keeps the network layer as defence in depth, not as the identity claim.
