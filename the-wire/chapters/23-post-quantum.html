Post-quantum and what comes next

Chapter 5 built asymmetric cryptography on trapdoor functions — easy forward, infeasible to invert. A sufficiently large quantum computer changes what "infeasible" means for two of the three trapdoors this course has relied on throughout. Nothing about symmetric cryptography breaks. Almost everything about key exchange and signatures does, eventually — and the eventually is closer than the migration timeline is comfortable with.

23.1 What actually changes, and what does not

Two quantum algorithms matter here, and they attack completely different things.

AlgorithmAttacksEffect
Shor's algorithm (1994)Integer factorisation and discrete logarithmTotal break. RSA, Diffie–Hellman, and every elliptic curve scheme in this course become solvable in polynomial time.
Grover's algorithm (1996)Unstructured search — brute-forcing a keyQuadratic speedup only. Halves the effective key length, not a total break.

This is why the fallout is so asymmetric. Shor's algorithm does not weaken RSA-2048 — it makes factoring it as easy as multiplying two primes was in Chapter 5, which is to say, trivial. There is no "RSA-4096 is fine for now" response to Shor's algorithm the way there was to classical factoring improvements; the entire trapdoor collapses. Grover's algorithm is a genuine but survivable threat: AES-256 under Grover behaves like AES-128 does today, which is still comfortably secure. AES-128 under Grover is more concerning, which is part of why AES-256 is the conservative recommendation now.

Map this against Chapter 5's table

Recall the trapdoor functions from Chapter 5: RSA's hardness is factoring, Diffie–Hellman's and ECC's is the discrete logarithm. Both are exactly what Shor's algorithm solves. Chapter 5's entire asymmetric toolkit — key exchange, signatures, everything in Part II built on a trapdoor except the symmetric primitives in Chapter 4 — needs replacing. Hashing (Ch. 3) and symmetric encryption (Ch. 4) do not, beyond doubling key sizes where Grover applies.

23.2 The timeline that actually matters

The honest answer to "when will this happen" is that nobody knows — current large-scale quantum computers are nowhere near the qubit counts and error rates required to run Shor's algorithm against a 2048-bit RSA key. Estimates from serious researchers range from the 2030s to considerably later, and the uncertainty itself is not the reason to act.

The reason to act is this asymmetry, and it is worth working through concretely:

Interactive · harvest now, decrypt later—
An attacker recording encrypted traffic today does not need a quantum computer now. They need one before your data stops mattering. Move the sliders and watch how little margin some categories have.

This is not speculative. Nation-state actors are widely assessed to already be recording high-value encrypted traffic for exactly this purpose. It costs storage, which is cheap, against a payoff that may take a decade to materialise — a trade well within the resources of a state intelligence service. The traffic being recorded today, protected by classical ECDHE, is not retroactively protected by any future upgrade. Forward secrecy (Ch. 6) defends against a compromised server key. It does nothing against an algorithm that becomes solvable.

23.3 The new trapdoors

NIST ran an open, multi-round public competition — the same model that produced AES — and standardised the winners in August 2024. All of them are built on lattice problems, a different mathematical family than factoring or discrete logarithms, believed hard even for a quantum computer.

StandardReplacesProblemPublic keySignature/ciphertext
ML-KEM (FIPS 203, ex-Kyber)ECDHE key exchange (Ch. 6)Module Learning With Errors~800–1,600 B~800–1,600 B
ML-DSA (FIPS 204, ex-Dilithium)ECDSA / Ed25519 (Ch. 7)Module LWE + SIS~1,300–2,600 B~2,400–4,600 B
SLH-DSA (FIPS 205, ex-SPHINCS+)Signatures, hash-based fallbackHash function security only~32–64 B~8,000–50,000 B

The size column is the immediate practical concern. Chapter 5 noted X25519 keys at 32 bytes; ML-KEM keys start around 800 bytes, over twenty times larger. In a TLS handshake this matters directly — Chapter 13's HelloRetryRequest becomes more likely when packets approach fragmentation thresholds, and Chapter 12's decade-old ossification problem resurfaces as middleboxes choke on ClientHello messages larger than they have ever seen.

Why the lattice-based schemes, and why keep SLH-DSA around

Learning With Errors reduces to problems believed hard on both classical and quantum computers, and three decades of cryptanalysis have not meaningfully dented the core assumption — though this is a far shorter track record than RSA's, and post-quantum schemes are newer as a field. SLH-DSA exists as an insurance policy: its security rests entirely on hash function collision resistance (Ch. 3), a much better-understood and more conservative assumption, at the cost of enormous signatures. If ML-DSA's lattice assumption is ever broken, SLH-DSA is the fallback nobody had to invent under pressure.

23.4 Hybrid, not a replacement — yet

Nobody is deploying pure post-quantum key exchange in production TLS today. The deployed pattern is hybrid: combine a classical exchange and a post-quantum one, and derive the session key from both.

Interactive · hybrid key exchangeBoth must fail
The shared secret is HKDF(X25519_secret ‖ ML-KEM_secret) — Chapter 13's key schedule, extended by one more input. An attacker needs to break both trapdoors to recover anything.
Group name in a TLS 1.3 ClientHello key_share:  X25519MLKEM768

shared_secret = HKDF-Extract(
    salt = 0,
    ikm  = X25519_shared_secret || ML_KEM_768_shared_secret
)
   \u2014 exactly Chapter 13\u2019s key schedule, with one more input folded in

This is the conservative engineering answer to a field that is, relative to RSA's fifty years of scrutiny, young: it costs one extra key exchange's worth of computation and bandwidth, and it fails safe. If ML-KEM turns out to have an undiscovered classical weakness, X25519 still protects the connection against everyone except a quantum adversary. If a quantum computer arrives before classical cryptanalysis finds a flaw in ML-KEM, the lattice component still holds. Breaking hybrid requires breaking both — Chrome, Firefox, and major cloud providers have been shipping X25519MLKEM768 as a negotiable group since 2024, which you may have already exercised in Chapter 13's lab.

23.5 What has no answer yet

Key exchange and signatures both have shipped, standardised, deployed replacements. Two things do not:

  • Fully homomorphic encryption — computing directly on encrypted data — remains far too slow for general production use, though it is advancing and already viable for narrow, high-value use cases.
  • Truly quantum-resistant hash-based signature efficiency — SLH-DSA works today but at a size cost that makes it impractical for high-frequency signing, and is used only as a fallback where its more conservative security assumption is worth the overhead.

Knowledge check

Why does Shor's algorithm threaten RSA and ECC far more severely than Grover's algorithm threatens AES?

This maps directly onto Chapter 5's trapdoor functions. Factoring and discrete log are exactly what Shor's algorithm solves efficiently, collapsing the entire trapdoor. Grover's algorithm generically speeds up brute-force search by a quadratic factor, which is precisely offset by doubling the key length — AES-256 under Grover is roughly as strong as AES-128 is today.

An organisation encrypts medical records with classical ECDHE-only TLS today. Why is "we'll migrate when quantum computers are a real threat" an incomplete plan?

Harvest-now-decrypt-later. The migration only needs to be complete before the data stops being sensitive, not before quantum computers arrive — and for data that must stay confidential for twenty or fifty years, that deadline may already be uncomfortably close, even under conservative quantum timelines.

In hybrid key exchange (X25519MLKEM768), what does an attacker need to recover the shared secret?

The HKDF derivation folds in both shared secrets, so compromising only one trapdoor yields nothing. This is the conservative engineering answer for a newer field: it fails safe against either a quantum adversary or an undiscovered classical weakness in the lattice scheme, at the cost of one extra exchange.

Why does SLH-DSA remain standardised alongside ML-DSA despite far larger signatures?

SLH-DSA is the insurance policy. If ML-DSA's lattice assumption were ever broken, SLH-DSA — depending only on the hash properties covered in Chapter 3 — would already be standardised and ready, rather than needing to be designed and vetted under pressure after the fact.

23.8 Carry forward

  • Shor's algorithm breaks factoring and discrete log outright; Grover's algorithm only halves symmetric key strength.
  • Every asymmetric trapdoor from Part II — RSA, DH, ECC — is affected. Hashing and symmetric encryption are not, beyond doubling key sizes.
  • Harvest-now-decrypt-later means the deadline is when your data stops mattering, not when quantum computers arrive.
  • ML-KEM and ML-DSA are standardised, lattice-based replacements; SLH-DSA is the conservative hash-based fallback.
  • Hybrid combines classical and post-quantum exchange so breaking either alone yields nothing — the deployed answer today.
  • Enable hybrid key exchange now. Full migration of signatures and PQ-only deployment can follow more gradually.
