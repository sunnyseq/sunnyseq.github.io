Certificate lifecycle

A certificate is a claim with an expiry date, and the whole system rests on one question: what did the CA actually check before signing? For the overwhelming majority of the web, the answer is a single automated proof of domain control — which is both weaker and more robust than people expect.

10.1 The certificate signing request

Issuance begins with a CSR, and the thing to understand about a CSR is what it does not contain: the private key never leaves your machine. A CSR carries the public key plus the identity you are requesting, self-signed with the matching private key.

CertificationRequestInfo ::= SEQUENCE {
    version              INTEGER (0),
    subject              Name,                    -- often nearly empty now
    subjectPKInfo        SubjectPublicKeyInfo,    -- your public key
    attributes           [0] Attributes           -- requested SANs live here
}
-- then signed with the corresponding private key

That self-signature is a proof of possession. It stops someone requesting a certificate for a public key they do not control — which would otherwise let an attacker get a CA to bind your key to their name, or vice versa.

The CA is not obliged to honour your CSR

A common misconception is that the CSR determines the certificate. It does not. The CA takes your public key, ignores most of what you asked for, and issues what its policy allows. Public CAs routinely discard the Subject fields entirely and set SANs only for names you proved control of. If you request CN=Totally Legit Bank, you get a certificate with no such field. The CSR is a request, and the only part that is structurally binding is the public key.

10.2 What the CA validates

TypeWhat is checkedTypical timeWhat the browser shows

EV — Extended ValidationDomain control plus a heavier legal, physical and operational checkDays to weeksPadlock

The right-hand column is the honest part. EV certificates once produced a green bar with the company name; every major browser removed that treatment between 2019 and 2021, after research showed users did not notice it, could not interpret it, and were not protected by it — while attackers could obtain EV certificates for legitimately-registered shell companies with confusingly similar names. Today EV is a paid assurance to the site operator, not a signal to the visitor. **DV secures the connection exactly as well.**

This is worth being precise about, because it is a common conflation: a certificate authenticates a domain name, not a business, and never a business's honesty. A phishing site at bank-secure-login.example gets a perfectly valid DV certificate in seconds. The padlock says your connection to that server is private and unmodified. It says nothing at all about whether you should be talking to it.

10.3 ACME, and proving domain control

ACME (RFC 8555) is the protocol that automated all of this, and it is the reason HTTPS went from roughly 40% to over 95% of web traffic in a decade. The core insight: proving domain control is a task a machine can do, so no human needs to be involved on either side.

DNS-01 in Kubernetes

DNS-01 is usually the right choice inside a cluster, because it needs no inbound connectivity at all — which means you can obtain publicly-trusted certificates for services that are not publicly reachable. The trade-off is real and worth stating plainly: whatever holds those DNS API credentials can issue certificates for every name in the zone. Scope the credential to a single delegated zone used only for challenges, not to your production DNS.

10.5 CAA — telling CAs to refuse

Chapter 8's uncomfortable fact was that any of ~150 CAs can issue for your domain. CAA is the partial fix: a DNS record naming which CAs are permitted. Checking it has been mandatory for all public CAs since September 2017.

example.com.  IN CAA 0 issue     "letsencrypt.org"
example.com.  IN CAA 0 issuewild "letsencrypt.org"
example.com.  IN CAA 0 iodef     "mailto:security@example.com"

; lock issuance to one account, not just one CA:
example.com.  IN CAA 0 issue     "letsencrypt.org; accounturi=https://acme-v02.api.letsencrypt.org/acme/acct/12345"

; forbid everything:
example.com.  IN CAA 0 issue     ";"

Two things to understand about its limits. CAA is checked by the CA at issuance time, not by the browser at connection time — so it prevents misissuance rather than detecting it, and it does nothing about certificates issued before you set the record. And it depends on the CA resolving DNS honestly, which an attacker who controls resolution during validation can undermine. That is why CAA, multi-perspective validation and Certificate Transparency are complementary rather than alternatives.

10.6 Renewal, and why lifetimes keep shrinking

Public TLS certificate lifetimes have been ratcheting down steadily: 5 years, then 3, then 825 days, then 398. A CA/Browser Forum ballot passed in 2025 sets a staged reduction to 200 days in March 2026, 100 days in March 2027, and 47 days in March 2029.

The reasoning is Chapter 11's subject in advance: revocation does not really work, so a short lifetime is the fallback. A compromised key that expires in 47 days is a bounded problem; one valid for three years is not. Domain ownership also changes, and a certificate outliving the owner's control of the name is a live risk.

The operational consequence is not subtle. At 47 days, manual renewal is not a process anyone can sustain — you renew at roughly a third of the lifetime remaining, so every certificate needs attention every two weeks. Automation stops being good practice and becomes the only practice.

Renewal is not reissuance of the same certificate

Worth stating because it causes confusion: renewal produces an entirely new certificate with a new serial and new validity dates. Whether it also has a new key is your choice, and rotating the key at renewal is the stronger option — it bounds the value of a key compromise you have not yet noticed.

The failure modes worth monitoring for
  • Renewal succeeds, reload does not. The new certificate is on disk and the process is still serving the old one from memory. Monitor what the server presents on the wire, never what is in the filesystem.
  • Rate limits. Let's Encrypt allows 50 certificates per registered domain per week and 5 duplicates per week. A crash-looping client burns through that quickly and then cannot renew for days. Use the staging environment while testing.
  • Certificates nobody owns. A load balancer someone configured by hand three years ago. Discovery via Certificate Transparency logs (Ch. 11) finds these — you can enumerate every certificate ever issued for your domains, including ones you did not know about.
  • Clock skew. A certificate valid from 10 seconds in the future fails on a client whose clock runs slow. CAs backdate notBefore by an hour for exactly this reason.

10.7 Private CA issuance

Internal PKI follows the same lifecycle with the trust decision moved in-house, and the parameters change accordingly. Service mesh certificates commonly live hours, not months, and identify a workload rather than a hostname:

SAN: URI:spiffe://cluster.local/ns/payments/sa/api-server
Validity: 24 hours
Rotation: automatic, by the mesh control plane, no restart

At that lifetime revocation becomes irrelevant — a stolen key is useless within a day, which is a far stronger guarantee than any CRL or OCSP deployment achieves. This is the clearest practical demonstration of the principle behind the public web's shrinking lifetimes, just taken to its logical end.

Knowledge check

What does a CSR contain?

The private key never leaves your machine — a CA that asks for it is doing something badly wrong. The self-signature proves you hold the private key matching the public key you submitted, which prevents requesting a certificate for someone else's key.

You need a certificate for *.internal.example.com, on hosts with no inbound internet connectivity. Which challenge?

Two constraints both point the same way. DNS-01 is the only challenge that can issue wildcards, and it is the only one needing no inbound connectivity — the proof is placed in DNS rather than on the host. The cost is that the DNS API credential becomes a certificate-issuing credential, so scope it to a delegated challenge zone.

A phishing site has a valid DV certificate and shows a padlock. What has been proved?

DV proves domain control and nothing more, and it is automated and free. The padlock is a statement about the channel, not about the counterparty. This is also why browsers dropped the EV green bar — it implied a judgement about trustworthiness that certificates cannot make.

Why are public certificate lifetimes being reduced toward 47 days?

Chapter 11 covers why CRLs and OCSP largely fail to protect users. Given that, expiry is the revocation mechanism that actually works — it requires no network call, no responder, and cannot be soft-failed. The trade-off is that manual renewal becomes impossible, which is the point: it forces automation.

10.9 Carry forward

  • The CSR carries the public key and a proof of possession. The CA issues what its policy permits, not what you asked for.
  • DV, OV and EV differ in what was checked about the operator. Cryptographically they are identical, and browsers no longer distinguish them.
  • ACME challenges all answer the same question — control of what the name points at — by different routes. Only DNS-01 does wildcards.
  • CAA constrains issuance at the CA, before the fact. It is not checked by browsers.
  • Lifetimes are shrinking because revocation does not work. Automation is now mandatory, not optional.
  • Monitor what the server presents on the wire, not what is on disk.

Which leaves the question this chapter kept deferring: when a certificate must be invalidated before it expires, what actually happens?
