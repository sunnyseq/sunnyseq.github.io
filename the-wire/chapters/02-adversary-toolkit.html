The adversary's toolkit

An attack is a position plus a capability. Most people learn the capabilities and skip the positions, which is why "we use HTTPS" gets offered as an answer to threats HTTPS does not touch. This chapter puts an attacker at each point on a real path and asks the only question that matters: what does standing here actually buy you?

2.1 The path a request really takes

A browser in Mumbai requesting https://bank.example/balance does not talk to a server. It talks to roughly nine intermediaries, each of which is a physical box owned by someone, running software with bugs, in a jurisdiction with laws.

2.2 Passive versus active, precisely

The single most important distinction in attack classification, because it determines which cryptographic property you need.

A passive attacker copies bytes. They are undetectable in principle — reading a fibre tap or a mirrored switch port leaves no trace on the wire, changes no timing, drops no packet. You cannot detect them, so you must make their copy worthless. That is what encryption is for, and it is the only thing encryption alone is for.

An active attacker changes the byte stream. They are, in principle, always detectable, because a modification is a deviation from what the honest sender produced. So the defence is not prevention but reliable detection: authenticate everything, and treat a failed check as fatal rather than as a warning. This is why a TLS record with a bad tag terminates the connection outright instead of being retried — retrying an unauthenticated failure is how padding-oracle attacks (Chapter 4) get their oracle.

Rule of thumb

Passive attackers are defeated by confidentiality. Active attackers are defeated by integrity plus freshness. Every protocol that got this wrong got it wrong by having one and assuming it implied the other.

2.3 How an attacker gets on the path

Being on the path is not a metaphor. There are a finite number of concrete mechanisms, and they are worth knowing by name because the defences differ.

Layer 2 — the local segment

ARP spoofing. ARP has no authentication at all: any host on the segment can broadcast "the gateway's IP is at my MAC address" and every other host will believe it and update its cache. Two of these, one in each direction, and you are transparently in the middle of every conversation on the LAN. Mitigations are switch-level (dynamic ARP inspection, port security), not host-level.

Rogue access point / evil twin. Broadcast an SSID matching one the victim's device has saved. Many devices auto-associate. On open Wi-Fi you now hold the whole path. This is the single most common practical MITM position in the world, and it is why the "is the café Wi-Fi safe?" question has the answer "assume it is hostile and rely on TLS, because you cannot tell."

DHCP starvation and rogue DHCP. Exhaust the legitimate server's pool, then answer the next request yourself, handing out your own address as both gateway and DNS resolver. You now control routing and name resolution for that client.

Layer 3 — routing

BGP hijacking. The internet's routing protocol was designed among mutually trusting academic networks and is authenticated by convention rather than cryptography. An AS announcing a more specific prefix than the legitimate owner attracts that traffic globally. This is not theoretical: in April 2018, an AS announced Amazon's Route 53 prefixes and used the position to serve a fraudulent certificate for a cryptocurrency wallet site, stealing roughly $150,000 in about two hours. RPKI route origin validation is the partial fix, deployed unevenly.

Naming

DNS spoofing and cache poisoning. Classic DNS is UDP, unauthenticated, and answers are accepted if they match a 16-bit transaction ID and the source port. Kaminsky's 2008 attack made poisoning practical enough to force source-port randomisation across the internet in a coordinated emergency. DNSSEC signs records; DoH and DoT encrypt the query to the resolver. They solve different halves — see Chapter 20.

Why DNS attacks still matter under TLS

Poisoning DNS does not let an attacker forge a certificate. But it does let them obtain a real one: the ACME HTTP-01 and DNS-01 challenges (Chapter 10) prove control of a domain by answering at the address DNS points to. An attacker who controls resolution during validation gets a genuine, publicly-trusted certificate for a domain they do not own. Certificate Transparency (Chapter 11) is what makes this visible after the fact — and multi-perspective validation is what makes it harder in the first place.

2.4 Replay, and why freshness is a separate property

A replayed message is perfectly authentic. Every signature verifies, every MAC checks out, because it genuinely was produced by the legitimate sender. Integrity is intact and the attack still works. Consider a captured, correctly-authenticated transfer 500 to account 9931: resending it fifty times produces fifty valid transfers.

Freshness therefore needs its own mechanism, and there are only three real ones:

MechanismHow it worksCostWhere you'll meet it
Nonce / challengeReceiver sends an unpredictable value; sender must include it in the responseAn extra round tripTLS Random fields, WebAuthn challenge
Sequence numberMonotonic counter, receiver rejects anything not strictly greaterState must be kept, and must survive restartsTLS record layer, IPsec ESP
TimestampReject anything outside a windowRequires clock sync; window is a replay opportunityKerberos, JWT exp/iat, OCSP

This is exactly the reason TLS 1.3's 0-RTT early data carries a hazard warning. Early data is sent before the handshake completes, so the server has no fresh value from this connection to bind it to — an attacker can capture and resend it. The specification's answer is a discipline, not a mechanism: only send idempotent requests in early data. Chapter 13 covers the anti-replay measures that reduce, but do not eliminate, the window.

2.5 Downgrade attacks

Protocols support old versions and weak options for compatibility. An active attacker sitting in the middle of a negotiation can strip the strong options from the client's offer, leaving the server to "agree" on the weakest thing both sides support. Nobody was compromised; both endpoints simply did what they were told.

Three real examples worth knowing:

  • SSL stripping (Moxie Marlinspike, 2009). The user types bank.com, which is plain HTTP before the redirect. The attacker intercepts the redirect and serves HTTP to the victim while speaking HTTPS to the server. The padlock never appears, and most users never look. HSTS exists specifically to kill this: the browser refuses plaintext for a known host, and the preload list closes the very first visit.
  • FREAK / Logjam (2015). Legacy "export-grade" 512-bit key exchange, mandated by 1990s US export rules and never fully removed, could be forced by a MITM and then broken offline in hours. A twenty-year-old policy compromise became a live vulnerability.
  • POODLE (2014). Browsers retried failed handshakes at lower versions to work around broken servers. An attacker just had to make the good handshake fail. The fallback itself was the vulnerability — hence TLS_FALLBACK_SCSV, and TLS 1.3's decision to remove negotiation flexibility rather than police it.
The structural lesson

Negotiation must be authenticated by the handshake it produces. TLS does this with the Finished message, which is a MAC over the full transcript of everything both parties said — including the version and cipher lists. If an attacker altered a single byte of the offer, the two sides compute different transcript hashes and the connection dies. Chapter 12 walks through this byte by byte; it is the most elegant idea in the protocol.

2.6 Side channels

The formal model assumes the attacker sees only inputs and outputs. Real hardware also emits time, power, electromagnetic radiation, sound, and cache state. If any of these depend on the secret, the secret leaks — and no amount of algorithmic strength helps, because the algorithm is not what is leaking.

The canonical failure is a non-constant-time comparison:

Python · a timing leak, and the fix
# WRONG — bails on the first differing byte.
# An attacker who can measure response time recovers the tag byte by byte:
# 256 guesses per byte, not 256**32 for the whole tag.
def check(tag, expected):
    return tag == expected          # short-circuits

# RIGHT — always touches every byte, branches on nothing secret.
import hmac
def check(tag, expected):
    return hmac.compare_digest(tag, expected)

# Demonstrate the leak (run it; the gap is small but statistically obvious)
import time, statistics
secret = b"A"*32
def naive(g):
    for a, b in zip(g, secret):
        if a != b: return False
        time.sleep(0)               # stand-in for per-byte work
    return True
for prefix in (0, 8, 16, 24):
    g = b"A"*prefix + b"Z"*(32-prefix)
    ts = [ (lambda t0: (naive(g), time.perf_counter()-t0)[1])(time.perf_counter()) for _ in range(20000) ]
    print(prefix, "matching bytes ->", round(statistics.median(ts)*1e9), "ns")

The same principle scales up. Padding oracles (Chapter 4) leak plaintext when a decryption failure is distinguishable from a padding failure — by error message, by response code, or merely by how long the failure took. Cache-timing attacks on AES table lookups drove the design of AES-NI hardware instructions. Spectre and Meltdown turned speculative execution into a cross-boundary read primitive. The recurring lesson: never branch or index on secret data, and prefer library primitives written by people who have already thought about this.

2.7 Lab · watch a handshake happen

Before Part IV takes the handshake apart, see one for yourself. You will not understand every line yet — that is the point. Come back to this output after Chapter 13.

Shell · observe, capture, compare
# 1. Watch a full TLS handshake in text form
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com </dev/null 2>&1 | head -60

# 2. What does a *passive* attacker actually see? Capture the first packets.
#    The ClientHello is unencrypted — note the SNI field in cleartext.
sudo tcpdump -i any -n -s0 -A 'tcp port 443 and tcp[((tcp[12:1] & 0xf0) >> 2):1] = 0x16' -c 4

# 3. Force a downgrade and watch it be refused by a modern server
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -tls1_1 </dev/null 2>&1 | grep -iE 'protocol|alert|error'

# 4. Confirm HSTS is doing its job for a site that sets it
curl -sI https://github.com | grep -i strict-transport-security

Step 2 is the one to sit with. Even with TLS fully deployed, the server name you are visiting travels in cleartext in the ClientHello, because the server needs it to choose a certificate before any key exchange has happened. That chicken-and-egg problem is what Encrypted Client Hello solves, and it is covered in Chapter 14.

Knowledge check

An attacker records an authenticated API request and resends it later. Every signature verifies. Which property was missing?

Integrity held perfectly — the message was genuinely produced by the legitimate sender and arrived unmodified. What was missing is any binding to this moment. Freshness needs its own mechanism: a nonce, a sequence number, or a timestamp window.

A user is on a hostile Wi-Fi network and visits an HTTPS site with a valid certificate. Which of these does the attacker still learn?

The SNI extension in the ClientHello is cleartext, so the hostname is visible; so are the IP address, packet sizes and timing. The path, query, headers, cookies and body are all inside the encrypted record layer. Traffic analysis on sizes and timing alone can often identify which page was fetched, which is why padding and ECH exist.

Why does TLS include a MAC over the entire handshake transcript in the Finished message?

The transcript MAC is the anti-downgrade mechanism. Both sides independently hash everything that was said; if a middlebox stripped a strong cipher from the client's offer, the hashes differ and the handshake aborts. Key ownership is proved separately by CertificateVerify, and forward secrecy comes from ephemeral key exchange.

Which of these is a passive attack?

Harvest-now-decrypt-later is purely observational — no bytes are altered, nothing is injected, and it is undetectable on the wire. It is also the reason forward secrecy (Chapter 6) and post-quantum key exchange (Chapter 23) matter for traffic sent today. The other three all modify or inject.

2.8 What to carry into Part II

  • An attack is a position plus a capability. Name the position before you name the defence.
  • Passive attackers are invisible, so make their copy useless. Active attackers are visible, so check everything and fail hard.
  • Authenticity does not imply freshness. Replay is a separate problem with separate mechanisms.
  • Any negotiation an attacker can influence must be authenticated by the result of that negotiation.
  • Secrets leak through time and power, not only through outputs. Do not branch on secret data.
