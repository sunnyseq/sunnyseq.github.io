What security actually means

Before any cryptography, a claim: security is not a property of a system. It is a property of a system relative to a stated adversary. Change the adversary and the same system flips from secure to broken without a line of code changing.

1.1 The three properties

Almost every security requirement you will ever write reduces to one of three properties, traditionally called the CIA triad. They are worth stating precisely, because the imprecise versions cause real bugs.

PropertyPrecise statementPrimary mechanism
ConfidentialityAn adversary who observes the data learns nothing about the plaintext beyond its length and timing.Encryption (Ch. 4, 5)
IntegrityAny modification of the data is detected with overwhelming probability before the data is acted upon.MACs and signatures (Ch. 3, 7)
AvailabilityThe legitimate party can obtain the service within its stated bound, despite adversarial load.Capacity, rate limits, filtering (Ch. 20)

Note what confidentiality does not promise. Length and timing leak. TLS hides what you asked a server, but not that you asked, not how big the answer was, and not when. A great deal of practical traffic analysis lives in that gap, and Chapter 14 covers the partial fixes (padding, ECH).

Two properties are usually bolted onto the triad because the triad alone cannot express them:

  • Authentication — the party at the other end is who they claim to be. This is the property that pure cryptography cannot give you, and the reason Part III exists at all.
  • Non-repudiation — the party cannot later deny having sent the message. Signatures give this; MACs deliberately do not, because both sides hold the same key and either could have produced the tag.
The distinction that catches people

Encryption does not provide integrity. A classic CTR-mode ciphertext can be flipped bit-for-bit by an attacker who never learns the key: flip bit 7 of the ciphertext and bit 7 of the plaintext flips too. If your protocol encrypts amount=0100 and does not authenticate it, an attacker who knows the format can make it amount=9100. This single mistake is why AEAD (Chapter 4) exists and why "just encrypt it" is never a complete answer.

1.2 The threat model is the specification

A threat model answers four questions. If you cannot answer all four, you do not yet have a security requirement, you have a wish.

  1. What are we protecting? Name the asset concretely: session cookies, the signing key, patient records at rest.
  2. Who is the adversary and what can they do? Their capabilities, not their motives.
  3. What are we assuming they cannot do? The assumptions are the load-bearing part. Every real compromise is an assumption that turned out to be false.
  4. What does failure look like? What is the observable bad outcome you are buying insurance against.

Capability classes

Adversaries are classified by what they can do to the channel, not by who they are. This is the ladder, and each rung strictly contains the one below it.

ClassCan doReal exampleDefeated by
Passive / eavesdropperRead every byte in transitWi-Fi sniffing, tapped fibre, ISP loggingEncryption alone
Active / Dolev–YaoRead, drop, delay, reorder, replay, inject, forge source addressesRogue access point, ARP or BGP hijack, malicious middleboxEncryption + authentication + freshness
Endpoint-capableAll of the above, plus code execution on one endpointMalware, malicious browser extension, compromised CI runnerNothing on the wire helps. Isolation, HSMs, attestation.
Trust-anchor-capableCan issue a certificate any client will believeCompromised or coerced CA, enterprise TLS-inspecting proxy with its root installedCertificate Transparency, pinning (Ch. 11)

The Dolev–Yao model (1983) is the default assumption for all internet protocol design, and it is worth internalising in its strongest form: the network is the adversary. The attacker is not merely on the path, the attacker is the path. They deliver your packets as a courtesy. Every protocol in Part IV is designed against exactly this assumption.

RFC 3552 §3

The IETF's guidelines for writing security considerations formalise the same baseline: internet protocols must assume the attacker has complete control of the communication channel, and can read, modify, delete and inject at will. Anything weaker is not a valid assumption for a protocol that runs on the public internet.

1.3 Kerckhoffs's principle

Stated in 1883 for military ciphers, and still the sharpest line in the field: a cryptosystem must remain secure even if everything about the system, except the key, is public knowledge. Shannon's terser restatement is "the enemy knows the system."

This is not a moral position about open source. It is a practical claim about what can be replaced. Algorithms cannot be rotated: they are baked into hardware, libraries, standards and a decade of deployed devices. Keys can be rotated in seconds. So all of the secrecy must be concentrated in the part you can cheaply change. A design whose security depends on the algorithm staying secret has a single, unrotatable, permanently compromisable secret.

The practical test: if a design document leaking would break your system, the design is the problem.

1.4 Trust boundaries and the shape of a system

A trust boundary is any line across which data changes trust level. Data that was fully controlled by an attacker on one side becomes input to your logic on the other. Every serious vulnerability class in Chapter 18 — injection, deserialisation, SSRF — is a trust boundary that someone forgot to mark.

1.5 Defence in depth, honestly

Defence in depth is widely misquoted as "more layers is better." The accurate version is narrower and more useful: layers should fail independently. Two controls that share an assumption are one control with extra cost.

A worked example. You protect an admin endpoint with (a) a firewall rule allowing only the office IP range, and (b) a session cookie check. These fail independently — an attacker needs both a network position and a stolen session. Now replace (a) with "we check the X-Forwarded-For header for the office IP." Both controls now depend on the same assumption, that HTTP headers reaching the app are trustworthy. One forged header defeats both. You have two layers and one point of failure.

The design question is never "how many layers do we have." It is "name the single assumption whose failure defeats more than one of these."

Fail closed

Related, and cheap to get right: when a control cannot reach a decision, the default must deny. An authorisation service that times out and returns "allow" has converted an availability incident into a total authorisation bypass. The one deliberate exception is availability-critical safety systems, where a locked door in a fire is the worse outcome — and that exception should be written down, not stumbled into.

1.6 What cryptography does and does not buy you

Keep this list nearby for the rest of the course. Cryptography reduces problems; it does not eliminate them. Each primitive converts a large diffuse problem into a small concentrated one.

PrimitiveReduces this problem……to this one
Symmetric encryptionKeeping a large message secret across an untrusted networkKeeping a 32-byte key secret at both ends
MACDetecting modification of a large messageKeeping a 32-byte key secret at both ends
Key exchangeBoth ends needing a shared key without ever meetingBoth ends knowing they exchanged with the right party
SignatureProving authorship to anyoneEveryone knowing the correct public key
PKIEveryone knowing every correct public keyEveryone knowing a handful of correct root keys

Read the right-hand column downwards and you have the plot of this entire course. Each chapter's answer becomes the next chapter's question, and the sequence bottoms out at Part III with a genuinely unresolved problem: the root store on your device is trusted because your operating system vendor said so. There is no cryptographic step below that. It is a human and institutional trust decision, and Chapters 8 and 11 are about making that decision auditable rather than making it disappear.

1.7 Lab · look at what is already there

Every machine you use already contains the trust anchors this course will spend Part III unpacking. Count them before you have any theory about them.

Shell · inspect your trust store
# Debian/Ubuntu/WSL: how many organisations can vouch for any site you visit?
grep -c "BEGIN CERTIFICATE" /etc/ssl/certs/ca-certificates.crt

# List the ones your system will believe, by common name
awk -v cmd='openssl x509 -noout -subject' '/BEGIN/{close(cmd)};{print | cmd}' \
  /etc/ssl/certs/ca-certificates.crt | sed 's/.*CN *= *//' | sort -u | head -40

# macOS equivalent
security find-certificate -a /System/Library/Keychains/SystemRootCertificates.keychain \
  | grep -c "labl"

Typical answer: 130 to 150. Every one of those organisations can, technically, issue a certificate for your bank that your browser will accept without complaint. That is the actual size of the trust decision you make by opening a browser, and it is the problem Certificate Transparency was invented to make visible.

Knowledge check

A service encrypts its session tokens with AES-CTR but does not authenticate them. An attacker who has never seen the key modifies a ciphertext in transit. Which property has failed?

CTR mode is a stream cipher: ciphertext is plaintext XOR keystream. Flipping a ciphertext bit flips the same plaintext bit, so an attacker who knows the message format can make targeted edits without ever recovering the key. Confidentiality holds; integrity does not. This is precisely why AEAD modes exist.

Kerckhoffs's principle is best justified by which argument?

The last option is true but is not the core argument — it is about inevitability, not design. The principle is about where recovery is possible: a leaked key is a rotation, a leaked algorithm you depended on is an unrecoverable redesign across every deployed device.

Which pair of controls is not genuine defence in depth?

Both controls in the first option trust attacker-supplied HTTP headers. One forged request defeats both, so they share a single assumption and provide one layer at the cost of two. The others fail independently: different mechanisms, different assumptions, different attacker prerequisites.

Under the Dolev–Yao model, which capability may an attacker not be assumed to have?

Dolev–Yao gives the attacker total control of the network, but the endpoints remain honest and their internal state is private. Endpoint compromise is a strictly stronger model, and no transport protocol defends against it — which is why Chapter 21 moves keys into HSMs rather than trying to solve it on the wire.

1.8 What to carry into Chapter 2

  • Security claims are always relative to a stated adversary. "Is this secure?" is not a well-formed question.
  • Confidentiality, integrity and availability are distinct and require distinct mechanisms. Encryption alone gives you exactly one of them.
  • Authentication cannot be derived from cryptography alone — that gap is what Part III is for.
  • Assume the network is hostile by default, because on the public internet it demonstrably is.
  • Layers only count when they fail independently.
