Part IV  ·  TLS on the wire  ·  Chapter 12

The TLS 1.2 handshake

Everything from Parts II and III assembles here. Eleven chapters of primitives and trust machinery, arranged into a sequence of messages that turns a hostile network into an authenticated private channel — and every message in it exists because something would break without it.

12.1 What the handshake has to achieve

Four things, and it is worth naming them before looking at any bytes, because each message maps onto one:

  1. Agree parameters. Version, cipher suite, curve, extensions — negotiated over a channel an attacker can rewrite.
  2. Authenticate the server. Prove the peer holds the private key for a certificate naming the host you asked for. This is Chapter 8 through 11 arriving.
  3. Establish keys. Ephemeral key agreement giving forward secrecy, plus derivation into separate keys per direction and purpose.
  4. Verify the negotiation was not tampered with. A MAC over the entire transcript, which is what makes point 1 survive an active attacker.
Why study 1.2 when 1.3 exists?

Three reasons. It is still widely deployed — internal services, embedded devices, older load balancers. Its design mistakes are what motivated every deletion in 1.3, so 1.3 is unintelligible without it. And its message sequence is more explicit: 1.3 compresses and encrypts most of the handshake, which is better in production and worse for learning. Read this chapter, then Chapter 13 becomes a list of well-motivated subtractions.

12.3 Inside a ClientHello

The first message is cleartext, and it says a great deal about the client. This is what a capture actually looks like:

16 03 01 01 2c   TLS record: handshake(22), legacy version 3.1, length 0x012c
   01 00 01 28   ClientHello(1), length 0x000128
      03 03      client_version 3,3  = TLS 1.2
      9f 3e ...  client_random, 32 bytes  <-- freshness. Contributes to key derivation.
      20 e1 ...  session_id, 32 bytes     <-- for resumption (Ch. 15)
      00 08      cipher_suites length
         13 01   TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256          } offered in preference
         c0 2b   ECDHE-ECDSA-AES128-GCM-SHA256   } order, most preferred
         c0 2f   ECDHE-RSA-AES128-GCM-SHA256     } first
         00 ff   TLS_EMPTY_RENEGOTIATION_INFO_SCSV
      01 00      compression_methods: null only  <-- see CRIME, below
      00 d7      extensions length
         00 00   server_name (SNI): "bank.example"      <-- CLEARTEXT
         00 0a   supported_groups: x25519, secp256r1
         00 0d   signature_algorithms: ecdsa_secp256r1_sha256, rsa_pss_rsae_sha256
         00 10   ALPN: h2, http/1.1
         00 05   status_request  <-- "please staple OCSP" (Ch. 11)
         00 17   extended_master_secret  <-- see the triple handshake fix, below

Two things worth dwelling on. SNI is in the clear, because the server needs to know which certificate to present before any keys exist — the chicken-and-egg problem Encrypted Client Hello solves (Ch. 14). And compression is offered as null only, because TLS-level compression enabled the CRIME attack: when a secret and attacker-controlled data are compressed together, the compressed length leaks how much they have in common, recovering the secret byte by byte. The fix was to delete the feature.

12.4 The two key exchange modes

TLS 1.2 supports both shapes from Chapter 5, and the difference is the most consequential thing in the protocol.

Static RSA (TLS_RSA_*)ECDHE (TLS_ECDHE_*)
ServerKeyExchangeNot sentSent, carrying the ephemeral public key and a signature over it
ClientKeyExchangePremaster secret, RSA-encrypted to the certificate keyThe client's ephemeral public key
Server proves key possession bySuccessfully decryptingSigning the ephemeral parameters
Forward secrecyNoYes
Vulnerable to Bleichenbacher / ROBOTYes — PKCS#1 v1.5 padding oracleNo

Static RSA is the one worth understanding as a cautionary tale. The client encrypts the premaster secret to the server's long-term public key, so anyone who ever obtains that key can decrypt every session they recorded — the harvest-now-decrypt-later scenario from Chapter 6. It also keeps PKCS#1 v1.5 decryption in the hot path, which is what Bleichenbacher's 1998 attack targeted and what ROBOT found still exploitable on major sites nineteen years later. TLS 1.3 removed it entirely; disable it in 1.2 configurations.

12.5 Key derivation

The raw ECDHE output is not used as a key. It goes through the PRF, which in TLS 1.2 is HMAC-based:

premaster_secret = ECDHE shared secret (Ch. 6)

master_secret = PRF(premaster_secret, "master secret",
                    client_random + server_random)[0..48]

key_block     = PRF(master_secret, "key expansion",
                    server_random + client_random)

               split into:
                 client_write_MAC_key    } only for CBC suites;
                 server_write_MAC_key    } AEAD suites skip these
                 client_write_key
                 server_write_key
                 client_write_IV
                 server_write_IV

Note the separate keys per direction. Reusing one key both ways enables reflection attacks, where an attacker replays the client's own records back at it. And note both randoms feed in, so neither side alone controls the result.

Extended master secret, and the triple handshake attack

The derivation above binds the master secret to the two randoms but not to the identity of the parties. In 2014 the triple handshake attack exploited this: an attacker could arrange for two different connections to share a master secret, then splice them together across a renegotiation and impersonate the client. The fix, RFC 7627, replaces the randoms with a hash of the full handshake transcript, binding the secret to everything that was actually said — including the certificate. Modern stacks negotiate extended_master_secret by default and increasingly refuse connections without it. If you disable it for compatibility with something old, understand what you are re-enabling.

12.6 Finished, and why the whole thing holds together

Chapter 2 claimed that negotiation must be authenticated by the negotiation's own result. This is where that happens.

verify_data = PRF(master_secret, "client finished",
                  Hash(all handshake messages so far))[0..12]

Both sides independently hash every handshake byte exchanged, then MAC it under a key derived from the shared secret. If a middlebox stripped a strong cipher suite from the ClientHello, downgraded the version, or altered the supported groups, the two transcripts differ, the verify_data values differ, and the connection dies before any application data flows.

Two properties make this work, and both matter:

  • It is encrypted. Finished is the first message sent under the new keys, so an attacker cannot compute or forge it without the master secret.
  • It covers everything. Not a summary, not the important fields — every byte of every handshake message, in order.

This is the most elegant idea in TLS: rather than trying to protect the negotiation while it happens, the protocol lets it happen in the clear and then proves afterwards that it happened honestly.

12.7 The costs TLS 1.3 removed

Reading the sequence above, the design debt is visible:

  • Two round trips before any application data. On a 100 ms link that is 200 ms of latency on every new connection, on top of TCP's own handshake.
  • The certificate travels in cleartext, so a passive observer learns which server you reached even beyond SNI.
  • Negotiable everything — including weak options retained for compatibility, which is the raw material for every downgrade attack in Chapter 2.
  • Non-AEAD suites permitted, keeping MAC-then-encrypt and CBC padding alive, and with them Lucky 13 and the padding-oracle family (Ch. 4, 17).
  • ChangeCipherSpec, a message that exists only to say "the next thing is encrypted" — redundant, and the vehicle for CVE-2014-0224, where OpenSSL accepted it early and could be forced into using zero-length keys.

Shell · watch a 1.2 handshake in detail
# Force TLS 1.2 and read the whole exchange
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com -tls1_2 </dev/null 2>&1 \
  | sed -n '1,60p'

# The negotiated parameters, in one place
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -tls1_2 </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | grep -E 'Protocol|Cipher|Server Temp Key|Peer signature|Master-Key|Session-ID:'
#   "Server Temp Key: X25519, 253 bits" = ECDHE was used, so forward secrecy holds.
#   Its absence means static RSA \u2014 no forward secrecy.

# Every handshake message, with state transitions
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -tls1_2 -state </dev/null 2>&1 | grep SSL_connect

# See the raw bytes of the messages
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -tls1_2 -msg </dev/null 2>&1 | head -60
#   Look for ">>> TLS 1.2 Handshake [length ...], ClientHello" and read the hex under it
#   against the annotated dump in section 12.3.

# Is extended_master_secret negotiated?
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -tls1_2 </dev/null 2>&1 | grep -i 'extended master'

# Try to force a non-forward-secret suite \u2014 a well-configured server refuses
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -tls1_2 -cipher 'kRSA' </dev/null 2>&1 \
  | grep -iE 'alert|no ciphers|handshake failure'

# Capture and inspect a real ClientHello on the wire
sudo tcpdump -i any -n -s0 -X 'tcp port 443 and tcp[((tcp[12:1] & 0xf0) >> 2):1] = 0x16' -c 2
#   The SNI hostname is readable in the ASCII column. That is the point of Ch. 14's ECH.

# What suites does your server actually offer, in what order?
nmap --script ssl-enum-ciphers -p 443 example.com 2>/dev/null | sed -n '/TLSv1.2/,/least strength/p'

Knowledge check

What does the Finished message protect against that nothing earlier in the handshake does?

Everything before Finished travels in the clear and is therefore rewritable. Finished is a MAC over the complete transcript, computed under the newly derived keys, so both sides prove they saw an identical conversation. Key possession is proved separately by the ServerKeyExchange signature.

A capture shows no ServerKeyExchange message. What does that tell you?

ServerKeyExchange carries the ephemeral public key and its signature, so it is only needed for ECDHE and DHE. With static RSA the client encrypts the premaster secret directly to the certificate's public key — meaning that one long-term key decrypts every recorded session. Its absence is the wire signature of a suite you should turn off.

Why does TLS derive separate write keys for client and server?

With one shared key, a record the client produced would decrypt and authenticate correctly if bounced back at the client. Direction-specific keys make a reflected record fail authentication. The same reasoning drives separate sequence numbers per direction.

Why was TLS-level compression removed from the protocol?

Compression length depends on how much the inputs have in common. An attacker who can inject guesses next to a secret cookie watches the record shrink when a guess is right, and recovers the secret byte by byte. There is no way to keep compression and fix this, so the feature was deleted — the same reasoning behind most of TLS 1.3's removals.

12.9 Carry forward

  • The handshake does four jobs: negotiate, authenticate, establish keys, and prove the negotiation was honest.
  • ServerKeyExchange plus CertificateVerify-equivalent signing is what closes Chapter 6's machine-in-the-middle gap.
  • Finished is a MAC over the entire transcript, sent encrypted. It is the anti-downgrade mechanism.
  • Static RSA has no forward secrecy and keeps a padding oracle in the hot path. Turn it off.
  • Extended master secret binds the keys to the transcript, not just to two nonces.
  • Every removal in TLS 1.3 traces to a specific failure in this design.
