24.3 What TLS never promised

Worth stating precisely at the end of a course about TLS: encryption in transit is one layer among many, and it is easy to over-credit it. A perfectly valid certificate, a flawless handshake, and AES-256-GCM protecting every byte does not prevent:

  • A phishing site with its own valid DV certificate (Ch. 10)
  • SQL injection once the request arrives (Ch. 18)
  • An authorization check that was never written (Ch. 19)
  • A secret sitting in an environment variable (Ch. 21)
  • A compromised dependency in the application's supply chain
  • An internal network that trusted its own topology (Ch. 1, 20, 22)

The padlock is real and it is doing real work — everything in Parts II through IV is genuinely running, genuinely protecting the channel. It was never the whole system, and treating it as such is the single most common category error in how organisations reason about security.

24.6 A final knowledge check

Across the whole course

A connection has a perfectly valid TLS 1.3 handshake, a certificate that chains correctly to a trusted root, and AES-256-GCM protecting every byte. The application is then compromised via SQL injection. What does this demonstrate?

This is Chapter 18's opening point, and the whole reason Part V exists after Part IV. Every primitive worked exactly as designed. The vulnerability was never in the channel — it was in how the application handled data once TLS had faithfully delivered it, unmodified, from attacker to database query.

Which single property recurs across Chapters 6, 15, 16, and 21 — ephemeral key exchange, session ticket rotation, mesh certificate lifetimes, and dynamic database credentials?

The same insight applied at four different layers: a secret that expires quickly is worth little to whoever steals it. Forward secrecy destroys exchange keys after the handshake; ticket rotation limits how much resumption traffic one key can expose; mesh certificates make revocation nearly irrelevant; dynamic credentials make a leaked password stale before anyone can use it.

Across the whole course, what is the closest thing to a unifying design principle?

Chapter 1 stated it first and every subsequent chapter demonstrated it: hashing reduces integrity to a fixed-size digest, key exchange reduces shared secrecy to two ephemeral values, PKI reduces universal trust to a handful of roots, and defence in depth only works when each layer's failure is independent of the others'. That is the thread connecting a Merkle tree in Chapter 11 to a NetworkPolicy in Chapter 22.

24.7 Where this leaves you

Two threads were left deliberately incomplete, because they are large enough to be their own courses and this library already has them.

  • Chapter 22 assumed you know what a namespace, a control loop, and a service actually are underneath the YAML. Inside Out covers that ground from the API server down to a packet reaching a pod.
  • Chapter 20 and Chapter 22's pod hardening assumed familiarity with cgroups, capabilities, and the isolation primitives containers are built from. Under the Hood covers those from the syscall boundary up — including building a container by hand in about fifty lines, the exact mechanism behind everything runc does.

Both were cross-linked throughout this course precisely because none of what you just read stands alone. A certificate chain means little without understanding the process that terminates it; a NetworkPolicy means little without understanding the kernel primitive enforcing it. That was true on chapter one and it is still true here.
