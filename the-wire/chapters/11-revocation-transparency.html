Revocation and transparency

A certificate says it is valid until a date. What if that stops being true first? This is the part of PKI that has never really worked, and the story of how the industry stopped trying to fix it and built something different instead.

11.1 The problem revocation cannot solve

A private key leaks. A domain changes hands. A CA discovers it misissued. The certificate is still cryptographically valid and still within its validity window, and every client in the world will accept it. You need to tell them not to.

The structural difficulty: the client is talking to the attacker. In the exact scenario where revocation matters most — a machine-in-the-middle holding a stolen key — the attacker controls the network path. Any revocation check that goes over that path can be blocked. And a check that can be blocked must decide what to do on failure:

  • Hard-fail — treat an unreachable responder as revoked. Secure, and it means a CA outage takes down every site that CA serves. Nobody ships this by default.
  • Soft-fail — treat an unreachable responder as valid. Available, and trivially defeated: the attacker simply drops the check. This is what almost everything does.
Adam Langley's verdict, and it still holds

Chrome's then-lead on TLS put it plainly in 2012: soft-fail revocation checking is like a seatbelt that snaps in an accident. It works fine except in exactly the circumstance you need it. Chrome subsequently disabled online revocation checks by default, and the fundamental analysis has not changed since — which is why the industry's real answer turned out to be short lifetimes (Ch. 10) rather than better revocation.

11.2 The three mechanisms

Interactive · revocation under attackSelect a mechanism
Toggle the attacker on and off against each mechanism. The pattern to notice: every mechanism that requires the client to reach a third party is defeated by an attacker who controls the client's network.

CRL — Certificate Revocation List

A signed list of revoked serial numbers, published by the CA and fetched from the URL in the crlDistributionPoints extension. Simple, and it scales badly: lists reached megabytes, must be re-downloaded whole, and are cached for hours or days — so a revocation is not visible until the cache expires. Modern browsers use aggregated, compressed pushes instead (Chrome's CRLSets, Mozilla's CRLite), shipped as part of browser updates rather than fetched per-connection. Those cover only a curated subset, chiefly CA and high-value revocations.

OCSP — Online Certificate Status Protocol

Ask about one certificate rather than downloading everything. Better on bandwidth, worse on nearly everything else: it adds a blocking round trip to a third party before the page loads, it fails soft, and it tells the CA every site every user visits, which is a substantial privacy leak. Let's Encrypt began winding down OCSP entirely in 2025, citing exactly the privacy cost and the operational burden for a control that barely functions.

OCSP stapling

The genuinely good idea. The server periodically fetches its own signed OCSP response and attaches it to the handshake in the status_request extension. The response is signed by the CA and timestamped, so the server cannot forge it or replay an old one indefinitely. No third-party connection from the client, no privacy leak, no extra latency. The remaining flaw: if the server simply omits the staple, most clients shrug and continue.

Must-staple

An extension in the certificate itself declaring that a staple is mandatory. A client seeing it must reject a handshake with no staple, closing the omission gap. It is correct and barely deployed, because it converts a stapling failure into an outage — and stapling failures are common enough that most operators decline the trade.

11.3 Certificate Transparency

Revocation answers "is this certificate still valid?" Transparency answers a different and, it turns out, more tractable question: "was this certificate ever supposed to exist?"

Google proposed CT after DigiNotar. The insight is that you cannot prevent a trusted CA from misissuing, but you can make misissuance impossible to hide. Every certificate must be submitted to public, append-only logs. Domain owners monitor those logs for their own names. A fraudulent certificate is discovered in hours rather than never.

Since 2018 Chrome, and later Safari, require CT proofs for all publicly-trusted certificates. In practice this means a certificate that is not logged is not usable on the web.

The append-only guarantee

A log is only useful if it cannot rewrite history. CT logs are Merkle trees: leaves are hashed certificates, each internal node is the hash of its two children, and the root hash commits to every leaf. Change any certificate and the root changes. Two proofs make the guarantee checkable:

  • Inclusion proof — this certificate is in the log with this root. Requires only log₂(n) hashes, not the whole log.
  • Consistency proof — this newer root contains everything the older root did, with items only appended. This is what makes the log append-only in a verifiable way.
Interactive · Merkle inclusion proof8 certificates logged
The audit path is highlighted: those are the only hashes a verifier needs. For a log holding a billion certificates, an inclusion proof is about 30 hashes — roughly a kilobyte — which is why this scales where downloading a revocation list does not.

SCTs and the precertificate trick

When a CA submits a certificate, the log returns a Signed Certificate Timestamp — a signed promise to include it within a stated Maximum Merge Delay, usually 24 hours. Clients check SCTs, typically requiring two or three from independent logs.

There is a chicken-and-egg problem: the SCT should be embedded in the certificate, but the certificate must exist before it can be logged. The solution is the precertificate — a copy carrying a special critical "poison" extension that makes it unusable for TLS. The CA logs the precertificate, receives SCTs, then issues the real certificate with those SCTs embedded and the poison removed. The poison extension being critical is what guarantees no client will ever accept the precertificate itself (Ch. 9's criticality flag doing real work).

CT as an operational tool

This is the most immediately useful thing in the chapter. Because every certificate for your domains is public, you can enumerate them — including ones issued by teams you did not know were issuing, and ones from a CA you never authorised. Set up monitoring on crt.sh or a CT monitoring service for your domains and you get free asset discovery plus misissuance alerting. It is also worth knowing the flip side: CT leaks internal hostnames. A certificate for jenkins-staging.internal.example.com is published to the world. Use a wildcard, or a private CA, for names you would rather not advertise.

The honest summary of Part III: the internet largely gave up on telling clients that a certificate went bad, and instead arranged for certificates to stop being valid so quickly that it rarely matters — while making issuance public so that abuse is caught. Both are engineering responses to the same admission: you cannot rely on reaching a third party over a network the attacker controls.

# Does this server staple an OCSP response?
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -status </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | sed -n '/OCSP response/,/Next Update/p'
#   "OCSP Response Status: successful" plus This/Next Update = stapling works.
#   "no response sent by server" = not stapling.

# Query an OCSP responder manually
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -showcerts </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | awk '/BEGIN CERT/{n++} n==1' > leaf.pem
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -showcerts </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | awk '/BEGIN CERT/{n++} n==2' > issuer.pem

OCSP_URL=$(openssl x509 -in leaf.pem -noout -ocsp_uri)
echo "responder: $OCSP_URL"
openssl ocsp -issuer issuer.pem -cert leaf.pem -url "$OCSP_URL" \
  -header "Host=$(echo $OCSP_URL | cut -d/ -f3)" -no_nonce -text 2>/dev/null | head -25
#   Note what just happened: you told the CA which site you are visiting.
#   Every browser doing this leaks the same thing. That is why OCSP is being retired.

# Where would a CRL come from, and how big is it?
openssl x509 -in leaf.pem -noout -ext crlDistributionPoints
CRL=$(openssl x509 -in leaf.pem -noout -ext crlDistributionPoints | grep -o 'http[^ ]*')
curl -s -o crl.der -w "CRL size: %{size_download} bytes\n" "$CRL" 2>/dev/null
openssl crl -in crl.der -inform DER -noout -text 2>/dev/null | head -12

# Is must-staple set? (OID 1.3.6.1.5.5.7.1.24)
openssl x509 -in leaf.pem -noout -text | grep -A1 '1.3.6.1.5.5.7.1.24' \
  || echo "no must-staple (this is the common case)"

# Certificate Transparency: the embedded SCTs
openssl x509 -in leaf.pem -noout -text | grep -A12 'CT Precertificate SCTs'

Knowledge check

Why is soft-fail revocation checking described as ineffective?

The failure is structural, not implementational. In precisely the scenario revocation exists for — a machine-in-the-middle with a compromised key — the attacker controls the path to the responder. Soft-fail means blocking the check is as good as a valid response. Hard-fail would fix it and makes every CA outage a global outage, so nobody ships it.

What advantage does OCSP stapling have over client-queried OCSP?

The server fetches its own status and attaches it to the handshake. The response is still CA-signed and timestamped, so the server cannot forge or indefinitely replay it. What stapling does not fix is omission — that requires must-staple.

What does a Merkle inclusion proof demonstrate?

Inclusion is all it proves. Whether the certificate should exist is a judgement made by the domain owner monitoring the log. The efficiency is the point: a proof against a billion-entry log is roughly 30 hashes, which is why this scales where fetching a revocation list does not.

Why does a CA log a precertificate rather than the final certificate?

Embedding an SCT changes the certificate, which would change what was logged. The precertificate is logged first, SCTs come back, and the real certificate is issued with them included. The poison extension is marked critical so that no client will ever accept the precertificate for TLS — Chapter 9's criticality mechanism doing exactly the job it was designed for.
